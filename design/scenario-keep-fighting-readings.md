## Sources on hand

### Gawande, *Being Mortal*, Introduction (Class 5)

Scanned PDF, read as page images 2 to 7.

- Medical training taught how to save lives, not how to tend to a death. Aging,
  frailty, and dying were nearly absent from the textbooks.
- **Ivan Ilyich, via Gawande's summary.** A 45-year-old magistrate falls off a
  stepladder and develops a pain in his side. Doctors cannot agree on a
  diagnosis and their remedies accomplish nothing. What tormented him most,
  Tolstoy writes, was "the deception, the lie" that he was not dying but simply
  ill and needed only to keep quiet and undergo treatment. Nobody around him
  could face the subject of his death, and that was his most profound pain.
- **Joseph Lazaroff.** A city administrator in his sixties with widely
  metastatic prostate cancer, fifty pounds lost, the cancer compressing his
  spinal cord. Offered comfort care or a dangerous spinal operation, he chose
  surgery. His words: "Don't you give up on me."
- His son pulled Gawande aside afterward. The mother had died on a ventilator,
  and the father had once said he never wanted that. Now he was adamant about
  doing everything.
- The operation was a technical success and he never recovered from it:
  respiratory failure, systemic infection, clots, then bleeding from the drugs
  treating the clots. On day fourteen the son said stop.
- **The thesis of the passage.** Gawande's regret is not that Lazaroff decided
  badly. It is that everyone avoided an honest conversation about his actual
  disease. The specific risks of each treatment were explained carefully; the
  reality of dying was never touched. If the patient was pursuing a delusion,
  so were his doctors. They offered another treatment instead of
  acknowledgment, comfort, or guidance.
- The operation could never have returned what he actually wanted, which was
  his strength, his continence, and the life he had before.
- Context: in 1945 most deaths happened at home; by the 1980s only 17 percent
  did. Dying moved into hospitals and nursing homes.

**What this grounds in the case:** the pressure to offer one more intervention
because it is easier than an honest conversation; a family member who knows the
patient's earlier wishes and is now saying the opposite; the difference between
explaining risk and telling the truth; measuring a plan against what the patient
actually wants rather than against survival.

### Kathleen Watt, Q&A with the course (Class 7) + *Rearranged* chapter index

The Q&A is full text. The second file is only a chapter index with one-line
descriptions, so it gives themes, not quotable prose.

- **Two teams, and why trust decided it.** One team explained the operation and
  its reconstruction options in full and left the remaining choice to her. The
  other belittled her concerns in about six sentences, insulted physicians at
  another institution, and told her to schedule the surgery. She could never
  have trusted the second team.
- A patient usually cannot evaluate technical skill, but can evaluate character.
  The qualities she names: integrity, patience, humility, inquisitiveness,
  transparency, thoroughness, attention to detail, courtesy, respect for women,
  willingness to share power, and willingness to communicate truthfully.
- **Caregiving includes interpreting a patient to herself**, confirming her
  sanity and quieting her fears.
- **The blur story.** Without her glasses she saw a smear of color across the
  room, decided it was two women whispering about her, and became distressed.
  It was a vase of fading flowers. Nobody checked the view from her line of
  sight; they recorded her as hallucinating, and their reports to her doctors
  were wrong. Her distress was real and was correctable by one small act of
  attention.
- Equilibrium came from adjusting what success and routine meant, against the
  evidence her life was actually presenting.

**What this grounds in the case:** distinguishing a patient's confusion from a
patient's correctable circumstance; what the record says about a patient versus
what is true; a clinician who explains fully and leaves the real choice with the
patient; a family's trust as something spent or earned in single conversations.

## Sources researched rather than supplied

The owner directed that no further sources would be provided and that these
should be researched instead (2026-07-29). Summary level only, so no passage
from any of them is quoted in the case.

- **Tolstoy, *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*.** The family and the physicians keep a
  collective arrangement that he is ill rather than dying, and that isolation
  hurts him more than the disease. His doctors never settle on a diagnosis. His
  wife is intermittently concerned, mostly when his illness disrupts her. The
  young butler Gerasim is the only person blunt about the death, and the only
  one who gives physical relief, holding Ivan's legs up on his shoulders. At the
  end Ivan feels pity for his family instead of hatred and his terror goes.
  **In the case:** Ellen's request is the arrangement. Every node asks whether
  you join it. The clinician can occupy Gerasim's position or refuse it.
- **Annabelle Gurwitch, *The End of My Life Is Killing Me*.** Essays following a
  stage 4 lung cancer diagnosis. She refuses the cancer warrior identity and
  calls herself a cancer slacker. Motto: no runs, no ribbons, no religion.
  **In the case:** Ellen calls Daniel a fighter. The word is placed on him
  rather than chosen by him, and the case never endorses it.
- **Martin Welsh, *Laugh to Death: My Rx for Dying Well with ALS*.** A physician
  diagnosed with ALS in 2004 who wrote update emails to family and turned them
  into a book, more often irreverent than solemn. He died in 2010.
  **In the case:** Daniel plans his own funeral and says so plainly. Meeting
  that is scored as care; filing it as a mood symptom is scored as a loss.
  NOTE: written without humour. The owner rejected a joking version outright
  (style guide 27k), so the agency shows in him directing it, not in wit.

## Rules carried into this case

- The one-pager governs the situation; these readings govern the substance.
- Never model a character closely on a real person. Kathleen Watt is a living
  author the class met; Joseph Lazaroff is Gawande's patient. Neither may be
  the template for this scenario's patient.
- Death is not automatically a bad outcome here. Clinical well-being in an
  end-of-life case means comfort, honesty, dignity, and alignment with what the
  patient wants, not survival.
- All writing follows `design/style-guide.md` without exception.
