## Meghan O'Rourke, *The Invisible Kingdom: Reimagining Chronic Illness*

Researched, not read in full. What the case is built on:

- A silent epidemic of chronic illness that is poorly understood, frequently
  marginalised, and often undiagnosed and unrecognised altogether.
- **Her doctors were dismissive once the labs came back normal.** They shrugged
  off symptoms when test results did not indicate anything physically wrong.
- She writes about the tendency to disbelieve sick people who lack a clear
  diagnosis and nonetheless insist that they are ill.
- She names the mechanism: the diagnostic gaze limited by unconscious bias,
  institutional pressure, and epistemic hierarchies.
- Autoimmune disease dismissed as imaginary or purely psychological. Her scope
  covers autoimmunity, post-treatment Lyme, and long COVID.

**In the case:** the disbelief does the damage, not the uncertainty. That is why
the worst ending is not the endless testing loop but the quiet discharge, where
investigation stops without a plan and Priya simply stops coming. It is also why
`ev-name`, saying plainly that you do not know, scores well: O'Rourke's
complaint is not that her doctors lacked an answer.

**Representation guard:** Priya Raman is not O'Rourke. O'Rourke is a writer and
poet with autoimmune illness and post-treatment Lyme; Priya is a warehouse team
leader with no literary life. Owner approved the divergence before writing.

## Scott Slovic, Swarnalatha Rangarajan and Vidya Sarveswaran, "Introduction: Toward a Medical-Environmental Humanities. Why Now?" (Class 3)

Full text on disk, from *The Bloomsbury Handbook to the Medical-Environmental
Humanities* (2022).

**Note for the record:** this is Scott Slovic, the ecocriticism scholar. Not
Paul Slovic, the decision psychologist behind psychic numbing and compassion
fade. I initially prepped the wrong Slovic; the owner's one-pager corrected it.

- The volume works from the inextricable linkage between human and environmental
  health and frailty.
- **Transcorporeality**, borrowed from material ecocriticism, emphasises the
  vulnerability of human bodies to contaminants released into the environment by
  industrial activity. Bodies are porous to what is around them.
- **Entanglement** as the dominant metaphor: landscapes, bodyscapes, and
  micro- and macro-biota treated as one system rather than separate ones.
- **Precarity and ecoprecarity**, via Nayar on Bhopal, where a chemical disaster
  keeps shaping lives for more than thirty years afterward.
- Rob Nixon's **slow violence** sits underneath all of it: harm that is gradual
  and dispersed and therefore is not perceived, or counted, as harm.

**In the case:** this is why asking what she works around and lives above is a
clinical question rather than a fringe one, and why nobody had written either
down in fourteen months of notes. The diesel forklifts on the night rotation and
the unit that used to be a dry cleaner are slow violence: real, gradual, and
matched to no billable test.

**Deliberate design choice, owner-approved:** the environmental thread does not
resolve into "it was the building all along." Asking scores well because it is
the question nobody asked, and occupational health records ventilation as within
limits on the day they attend. A hidden answer would undo the point of the case.

## Rules carried into this case

- A diagnosis is not the win condition. The best outcome is a plan that holds
  without one, uncertainty kept explicitly open, and a patient still willing to
  come back.
- A late diagnosis is reachable and is not the top outcome: it costs her eleven
  further investigations, two procedures, and money she does not have.
- The mental health referral is genuinely double-edged. Offered as one part of
  the plan it helps; offered as the explanation it ends her care.
- All writing follows `design/style-guide.md` without exception.
