# Writing style guide

**Living document.** Every note the project owner gives on the writing lands
here, in their words where possible, **and it is updated the moment a critique
is given, not at the end of a batch of work.** Re-read it before writing or
revising any case text, including epilogues and outcome summaries. This is the reference for authoring and
rewriting all case text. When it conflicts with anything else, this wins.

Updated 2026-07-28 after the first full markup pass on "Two patients, one
clinician" (8 Word comments + chat notes).

## Rules

### Prose

1. **Be direct, never quirky or weird.** No odd humanizing details.
   - ✗ "still grades practice essays for a neighbor's kid" — "so weird"
   - ✗ "a cough that got weird" — "like wtf is that"
   - ✓ "She came into the clinic yesterday with a concerning cough."
2. **No poetic constructions.** State facts as facts.
   - ✗ "Sepsis until proven otherwise, and the clock on antibiotics starts now"
     — "Genuinely what is this wording"
   - ✓ "Her risk of sepsis is rising by the minute if antibiotics are not
     started."
3. **No buzzwords.** Owner: "Hate the word triage — do NOT use these types of
   buzz words."
4. **No overly specific clinical jargon.** ✗ "febrile" → ✓ "fever".
5. **Cut staging minutiae.** Owner flagged "pressed against the nurses'
   station, scanning the ceiling" as too specific.
   - ✓ "Marcus Webb is out of bay seven again and is convinced that the staff
     are trying to hurt him."
6. **Far fewer details overall.** If a detail doesn't change the decision or
   the meaning, cut it.

### Names and roles

7. **Patients get names.** First and last is fine.
8. **The nurse is always "Nurse [Lastname]"** (e.g. Nurse Nair) — named, but
   the role is glued to every mention.
9. **The supervisor is "your supervisor" in passing.** Only in a direct
   one-on-one moment may it be "your supervisor, Dr. Kessler" — never bare
   "Dr. Kessler" in narration.
10. **Security likewise carries its role:** "Officer Boone, the security
    guard." Owner: "WAY TOO MANY NAMES… it is far more easy to read" when
    roles are stated.

### Character construction

11. **NEVER model a character closely on a real person.** Owner, on v1
    Marcus: "COMPLETELY REMAKE THIS CHARACTER — I do not want it to be nearly
    this close to Maury Ornest. This can come off as offensive." A severe
    mental illness (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder) may be depicted;
    biographical fingerprints of a real individual (the baseball career, the
    painting) may not. Composite, ordinary backgrounds only.
12. **Bios read as a direct intro line:** "Eleanor Vance: seventy-one, retired
    middle-school teacher who lives alone." Then one or two plain facts that
    matter to the story (the daughter who calls the desk is fine).

### Punctuation and sentence shapes (v2 notes)

13. **NO em dashes or en dashes. Anywhere.** Owner: "NO MORE EM EN OR WHATEVER
    DASHES." Use commas, periods, or a colon.
14. **Banned sentence structures**, owner's words: "NEVER do the 'I need BLANK
    not BLANK' sentence structure or the 'It's not X, it's Y,Z' sentence
    structure those are disgusting and terrible to read."
15. **No video-game phrasing.** ✗ "Eleanor still needs your decisions",
    "It's time to act". Owner: "This is not a video game."

### Dialogue (v2 notes)

16. **Use quotes sparingly.** Owner: "TOO MANY QUOTES you are literally doing
    too much, enough of these snarky quotes." Most decisions should be actions,
    not speeches. Keep only a few spoken lines per case.
17. **Dialogue uses the same plain register as everything else.** No valiant
    speeches, no clever lines. Owner on a rejected line: "These quotes sound
    tacky and cliché… not this crazy weird valiant answer." Their replacement:
    "These are both emergencies that need attention."
18. **No tacked-on flourishes** like "Your choice."

### Perspective passages (v2 notes)

19. **Perspectives are direct, not literary.** Owner: "This is too much like
    literature, I do not like it! Make it more direct, not like a creative
    writing piece. STOP TAKING SO MANY CREATIVE LIBERTIES." State what the
    person believes, wants, or fears in plain sentences. No atmosphere, no
    fragments for effect.

### Story structure

20. **Simpler storylines.** Fewer simultaneous threads and named actors per
    beat; the reader should never have to work out who is who.
21. **Do not dwell on harm to the nurse.** She is not part of the
    PATIENT / DOCTOR / INSTITUTION triangle. Keep her role functional.

### Stakes (v2 notes)

22. **Eleanor can die.** If the user ignores her needs across roughly four
    decisions, she dies. This is gated on clinical well-being reaching its
    floor.
23. **The doctor can be sued**, by Eleanor's daughter or Marcus's brother, when
    a patient is harmed, especially when one patient was clearly prioritized
    over the other. Gated on PATIENT at or near its lowest.
24. **The doctor can be fired.** Gated on DOCTOR or INSTITUTION at or near its
    lowest.
25. **Waiting room harm is an INSTITUTION consequence and should be
    exacerbated.** Low efficiency means people in the waiting room get sicker,
    and that can end the doctor's job.
26. **These are extremes.** They fire only when a stakeholder score sits at or
    near its absolute floor, never on a merely mediocre run.
27. **Epilogue voice:** patients are described in the **third person**; the
    clinician is addressed in the **second person**.
27a. **Epilogues state what happened. Nothing else.** They are not closing
    monologues and carry no moral. Owner: "I hate these descriptions. They are
    not direct and sound cliché funky weird and cringy… Simply answer 'what
    happened' to them, do not give them a whole complicated thing."
    - ✗ "Eleanor taught school for thirty-one years, so she knows what a room
      with too few adults looks like…"
    - ✓ "Eleanor was admitted for treatment of sepsis and recovered. She went
      home six days later."
    - ✓ "Marcus was taken out of the hospital by police and held overnight. He
      was never seen by a psychiatrist."
    Two or three plain sentences is the whole budget.
27b. **Every rule in this document applies to ALL generated text**, without
    exception: epilogues, aftermath and delayed outcomes, outcome summaries,
    results-screen copy, and interface text. Owner: "Literally any and all
    generated text should abide by our writing doc." Nothing is exempt for
    sitting at the end or being short.
27c. **Aftermath and delayed outcomes report facts, not scenes.** No time-stamp
    storytelling, no atmosphere, no implied narrator.
    - ✗ "Two weeks later your incident report has been escalated for review,
      and the review is of you. There is a meeting on your calendar with the
      program director and someone from the legal office."
    - ✓ "Your incident report was escalated for review. The review is of you.
      A meeting with the program director and the legal office is scheduled."
    State what happened and stop.
27d. **Every choice carries its own aftermath.** No aftermath text is shared
    between choices, so a player never sees the same consequence twice for two
    different decisions. If a new choice is added, it needs its own.
27e. **Do not write "the review is of you"** or similar reversals for effect.
    Say the plain version: "You are on thin ice with your program director."
27f. **The results screen shows no outcome-summary box.** It goes from "The
    encounter is over" straight to "How the encounter went". Endings are
    reported by the score rows and the epilogue, not by a closing line.
27g. **Every choice must show at least one gain and at least one cost.** Owner:
    "for every choice there should always be a positive AND negative in some
    categories." This is the Pareto rule made visible, and it has two halves:
    - The data must carry both signs, counting per-patient effects.
    - The interface must *draw* both. A cost stored only in `patientEffects`
      and never rendered makes the choice read as a free win, which is worse
      than having no cost at all.
    Patient chips are labelled with the patient's name, e.g. "Eleanor: Trust
    and relationship -1".
27h. **Rule 1 covers interface text.** The dashes in headings and buttons are
    held to the same standard as case prose.
27i. **Epilogue conditions cannot use `visited` on an ending node.** The engine
    records a path step when a node is *left*, so the terminal node the player
    lands on is never in the path and any such condition silently never fires.
    Key epilogue text off the same conditions the ending routing uses, and
    verify that each ending maps to exactly one reflection per character.
27j. **No metric effects on delayed outcomes delivered at an ending.** Routing
    resolves before those land, so an effect there can make the epilogue
    disagree with the ending the player was given.
28. **Reading connections are a plain "Inspired by" list in the app.** Titles
    only, no explanation of the link. The longer connection notes stay in the
    review exports for the author and reviewers.
29. **Patient expressions must match the patient's actual condition.** A
    deteriorating patient is never drawn looking relieved. The engine overrides
    the authored mood from the patient's own clinical well-being, so the
    picture cannot contradict the outcome.
30. **Multi-patient cases score each patient separately.** A case with two
    patients shows PATIENT: <name> twice, so strong care for one can never
    cancel out harm to the other. Attribute every patient metric to a named
    patient in `patientEffects`. Verify balance with `npm run check:paths`
    before shipping: catastrophic endings must be reachable, rare, and fire
    for the right reasons.

## What the owner approved (match this register)

- "Officer Boone, the security guard" — role stated with the name.
- "Let security handle it, go back to Eleanor and treat the patient you can
  treat." — "very well written… simple, clean direct."
- "Order an injection to sedate Marcus before the removal, so no police are
  needed." — "This is perfect."
- "Finish the antibiotics order first, about ninety seconds, then deal with
  Marcus." — "This is great."

The target is plain and operational, closer to a checklist item than to prose.

## Checklist before any draft goes to the owner

- [ ] Zero quirky details; every detail earns its place
- [ ] No poetic phrasing — read each sentence aloud; if it performs, rewrite it
- [ ] No buzzwords (triage, etc.), no jargon (febrile, etc.)
- [ ] Nurse = "Nurse X"; supervisor = "your supervisor"; security = role stated
- [ ] No character traceable to a real person
- [ ] Word counts well below v1 (v1 was 5,184 words / 14 nodes; aim roughly half)
- [ ] New versioned .docx produced
- [ ] No dashes in UI copy either, not just case text
- [ ] `npm run check:paths` reviewed for ending balance
